# Calibrating genome-wide convergence screens on the genomes they analyse

*Nature Computational Science version: Title, Abstract, Main (Introduction) and Discussion. Results, Methods, figures, Supplementary Information and references are as in the main manuscript.*

Alternative title: *Empirical calibration turns convergence screens into measurements: regressive gene loss in mammals*

## Abstract

Genome-wide screens for convergent evolution associate genes with traits that evolved repeatedly, but their false-call rates are usually inferred from simulations that share the method's assumptions. We present a convergence test calibrated on the genomes it analyses. It combines generalised least squares with a null covariance learned from all genes, an intersection–union requirement that every signal survive the removal of any independent origin, a simulated gene-loss null with species- and gene-specific annotation error, and empirical calibration of the complete pipeline on matched control traits, most held out until the method was fixed. In sequence simulations it controlled false discoveries under lineage-specific rate shifts where standard models did not. Across 15,703 genes in 115 mammals and four traits, it found no convergent rate shifts but recurrent gene losses in subterranean and marine mammals, with subterranean eye-gene losses robust to every check. Empirical calibration makes the reliability of each convergence call measurable.

## Main

When a trait evolves independently in several lineages, each origin is a natural replicate, and genes that change repeatedly across origins are candidates for its genetic basis \[1–3\], whether through identical substitutions, different changes in the same gene or the loss of the same function \[2,4\]. With hundreds of mammalian genomes now aligned \[5–7\], the question can be asked of every gene at once, and it becomes a computational one: an association problem across species, with species as samples, genes as features and a phylogeny that makes every sample dependent on the others.

Three families of methods have been built for it: tests for convergent shifts in evolutionary rate \[8–10\], for convergent amino-acid substitutions \[11,12\] and for convergent gene loss \[13,14\]. Gene loss is attractive computationally as well as biologically, because an inactivated gene is a discrete, repeatable event that marks a dispensable function \[15,16\]. These methods have produced a large catalogue of candidate genes for life in water \[8,17,18\], underground \[19\], at high altitude \[20\] and with biosonar \[21,22\], alongside gene-level cases such as paraoxonase 1 in marine mammals \[23\], the decay of smell, taste and vision \[24–26\] and hypoxia genes in highland humans \[27,28\].

What these screens rarely report is how often they would make a call if there were nothing to find. Three properties of the data make this rate hard to predict. Species are not independent samples \[29\]. Branch lengths estimated from sequences carry correlated errors, and a single lineage with an unusual rate can mimic convergence across several. Gene losses inferred from genome assemblies contain annotation errors concentrated in particular species and particular genes \[14,30\]. A null model that omits any of these will be miscalibrated, and simulations that share its omissions will not reveal it. Echolocation shows the consequence: a genome-wide signal of convergent substitutions \[11\] did not survive appropriate null models \[31,32\]. Phylogenetic permutations improve calibration \[33\] but are costly at genome scale and still assume that the trait is the only structure its lineages share.

Comparative biology also asks questions that a single P value cannot answer: how many origins are needed, when each change occurred and which of several correlated traits it reflects \[34\]. A screen can be calibrated and still credit a signal to the wrong trait. The difficulty grows with scale. Gene-loss classifications now cover hundreds of mammalian genomes \[30,35\], and the number of model-based methods for convergent substitutions and rate shifts continues to grow \[36,37\], yet their error rates are typically established on simulated data generated under the assumptions of the method being tested.

Here we treat calibration as a quantity to be measured on the data, not assumed. We developed a convergence test with four components. First, generalised least squares with a null covariance learned from all genes replaces an assumed Brownian-motion kernel, absorbing shared ancestry, lineage-specific rate jumps and correlated estimation error in one matrix that is estimated once and reused. Second, convergence is defined as replication: a leave-one-origin-out intersection–union test requires every signal to survive the removal of any origin, and an explicit composite null, simulated per gene, tests whether a single origin explains each called loss. Third, gene-loss evidence is calibrated against a generative null, an irreversible-loss model whose annotation error is the product of species and gene components, fitted to all genes jointly by vectorised pruning. Fourth, the complete pipeline, including covariance learning, null simulation and multiple-testing correction, is rerun on matched control traits, most of them held out until the method was fixed, so that its false-call rate is measured on the same genomes as its discoveries. We benchmarked the test on paired sequence simulations against naive tests, a phylogenetic mixed model and RERconverge, and on 500 control traits from real genomes.

Applied to 15,703 genes in 115 mammals from the Zoonomia alignment and TOGA annotations \[5,30\], for echolocation, marine life, subterranean life and high altitude (Fig. 1), the calibrated test found no convergent rate shifts but recurrent gene losses in subterranean and marine mammals. The measurement of calibration then graded these calls: subterranean losses of lens and retinal genes withstood every check, including a genome-wide test against single-origin explanations, whereas the marine set depended on how uncertain losses were coded and on a residual misfit of the loss model in marine species. Calibration measured on the data thus turns a list of candidates into a list with known reliability.

## Discussion

Genome-wide association across species faces the same problem as association within species: without a calibrated null, a screen reports structure, not signal. Here we built a convergence test whose false-call rate is measured on the genomes it analyses, and showed what such a test finds. Across 15,703 genes in 115 mammals and four traits, it detected no convergent rate shift and a convergent gene-loss signal only in marine and subterranean mammals; within these, subterranean eye-gene losses passed every check, including a genome-wide test against single-origin explanations, whereas the marine set depended on how uncertain losses were coded.

Four methodological lessons generalise beyond this application. First, a null covariance learned from all genes absorbed sources of dependence that a Brownian-motion kernel does not represent, including clade-level rate jumps and correlated estimation errors between neighbouring branches, and, together with the leave-one-origin-out requirement, kept the rate layers calibrated across levels of confounding in sequence simulations where naive tests and a Brownian phylogenetic mixed model were not (Extended Data Figs 1 and 3; Supplementary Notes 1–3). Second, defining convergence as replication turns a vague requirement into a testable one: leave-one-origin-out is an intersection–union test, and its complement, the explicit composite null that a single origin carries the loss, can be simulated directly. Third, the generative null must contain the error process of the data. Two loss nulls that looked reasonable failed on matched control traits, one with 28 calls in a single control trait, until annotation error was modelled as a product of species and gene components. Fourth, calibration is an empirical quantity. Running the complete pipeline on matched control traits, including covariance learning, null simulation and multiple-testing correction, and holding out most of these controls until the method was fixed, turned calibration from an assumption into a measurement.

The measurement was informative in both directions. It showed that a call was made in 13% of control traits, close to but above the nominal 10%, and that false calls concentrated in lineages with many annotated losses. It exposed a residual misfit of the loss model that is largest in marine species, which carry more losses than the fitted error model explains, and it showed that the marine call set changes when uncertain losses are recoded, whereas the subterranean eye-gene signal persists (Supplementary Notes 9 and 14). A screen that reports these sensitivities alongside its discoveries allows a reader to grade each call; one that reports only the calls does not.

The framework also answers questions that standard screens leave open. Joint models of correlated traits showed that most candidates of a combined hypoxia analysis were eye genes of subterranean species, so a composite trait can attribute a signal to the wrong mechanism \[34\]. Origin subsampling showed that the recovery of the subterranean losses depended on which origins were retained as well as on how many. State-based mapping placed each loss relative to the stem of its origin, separating single ancestral inactivations, as in cetaceans, from repeated ones, as for PON1 in pinnipeds. Biologically, convergence at the level of whole genes was regressive: functions lost underground and at sea, not new functions gained, which is consistent with the binary, repeatable signature of gene loss \[14,15,35\] and with the narrower, site-level or regulatory nature of most adaptive change \[21,22,27,28\].

The approach is computationally practical at genome scale. The covariance is estimated once and reused through sub-matrices for every leave-one-origin-out test, loss rates and fragilities of all genes are fitted jointly by vectorised pruning, and the control-trait and simulation analyses are independent, so they parallelise trivially. The same design accepts further layers without change to its calibration logic: site-level convergent substitutions \[12,36\], accelerated non-coding elements \[37\] or loss calls from other annotation pipelines.

Several limitations remain. Control traits cannot guarantee that every control lineage is biologically null, and their calibration need not transfer exactly to the focal traits. The loss layer inherits the annotation method and the human reference, so genes absent from human are untested, and neighbouring cluster genes, such as several olfactory receptors and type II keratins, may share one deletion, so counts of calls overstate the number of independent events. No adjustment was made across the five analyses and two codings, so the study-wide error rate exceeds the per-analysis 10%. The rate tests are conservative, and modest convergent shifts may be missed.

More broadly, convergence screens are increasingly applied to hundreds of genomes and many traits \[5–7,35\]. As their scale grows, so does the number of calls that structure, error and chance alone can produce. Measuring that number on the data at hand, rather than assuming it from simulation, is a small computational cost that changes what such screens can claim.
